# Task 1 Learning Outcomes

Revisiting encapsulation, abstraction and SOLID helped me give a more deliberate structure to Python concepts I was already using. I became more attentive to how responsibilities should be divided, where validation belongs and how shared state should be protected. These principles informed my first banking assessment, where monetary validation using `Decimal`, account-level locking and consistent transfer ordering supported a thread-safe implementation. The concurrent tests provided evidence that the design met its immediate requirements and established a foundation for further investigation.

The lecturer's feedback made that foundation more useful. While recognising the concurrency approach, the comments on traceability and scalability encouraged me to map requirements to tests and investigate lock contention across different thread counts. The balances remained correct, but separate account locks showed no sustained performance advantage. This distinction developed my understanding of software evaluation. I could justify the locking decisions through observed correctness while recognising that scalability requires its own evidence.

Later units on refactoring, TDD and dependency injection gave me different ways to challenge working code. In the [checkout exercise](../02-Coding-Exercises/SOLID-Checkout/README.md), separating payment responsibilities improved testability, while an additional boundary test exposed a fractional-penny error missed by six passing tests. Following that failure through to a rounding correction helped me appreciate how tests can guide improvement. It also made me more selective about abstraction, since additional classes or interfaces should address a credible requirement rather than complexity for its own sake.

That judgement carried into Strategy, Decorator, Visitor and Abstract Factory. These patterns enabled interchangeable workloads, separated recording from reporting and supported the assembly of related components. Their integration also showed how individual design choices could interact in unexpected ways when a balance changed without a corresponding record. Prechelt et al. (2001) found that simpler approaches sometimes performed better than patterns, reinforcing my view that flexibility must be evaluated alongside the coordination and recovery demands it introduces.

Studying software architecture, secure coding and AI-oriented design broadened this reasoning to the system level. In the capstone, opposing unrecorded transactions could pass reconciliation, illustrating the test oracle problem examined by Barr et al. (2015). Injecting `ReviewModel` allowed controlled testing of model-related decisions, while the Model Registry and Feature Store concepts helped me consider future model lifecycle and input management. Tabassi (2023) places trustworthy AI within a wider framework of governance and evaluation. These ideas have strengthened my view that reliable transaction evidence, recoverable operations and meaningful performance testing should underpin any further integration.

Working independently through the lecturecasts, readings and formative exercises helped me connect these stages of learning. I increasingly approached the original implementation as something to investigate and improve, using feedback and experimental evidence to reconsider earlier decisions. This has given me a stronger basis for evaluating how design principles contribute to secure, maintainable and dependable software.
